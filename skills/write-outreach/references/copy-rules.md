# Copy rules

The complete rule set behind `write-outreach`. Everything here is a default that a measured result in your own workspace may override. Record the override in `strategy/decisions.md` with the evidence.

## The buyer's brain

Four things are true of every cold recipient. Write for them, not for yourself.

1. **Skepticism is the default.** Not "maybe", a hard no. Their time, budget, and reputation are at stake. Disarm early: easy out, no pressure, no claim you cannot back.
2. **Attention is filtered.** The reader's filter is trained on obvious sales mail, length, and standard tactics. You have seconds and a preview line.
3. **Trust paradox.** Buyers want good solutions and hate being sold. Clear offer, human tone.
4. **Processing order.** Who is this and can I trust them? Is this relevant to me right now? What does answering cost me? Personalisation answers the first two. The CTA answers the third.

## Levers

Pick two or three per message. Never all of them. Each lever you add dilutes the others.

| Lever | What it does | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Relevance | "this is about me": a concrete detail of role, company, situation, placed in line 1 so it shows in the preview | always; this is the one non-negotiable lever |
| Curiosity | an open loop the reader wants to close, with enough clarity that tyre-kickers do not reply | you have a real finding about them |
| Social proof | peers first, neighbouring industry second, big logos last; irrelevant logos hurt | you have 2-3 nameable peers in their city or trade |
| Authority | articulate the pain better than they can; they will assume you have the fix | you have specific, sourced insight |
| Loss aversion | losses weigh more than gains; keep it subtle, never "offer ends Friday" | the trigger is a measurable leak |
| Reciprocity | give first; make the effort visible or gift a real asset | you can produce something for them in under an hour |
| Clarity | confuse and you lose; no jargon, no feature talk | always |
| Urgency | tie to a real trigger: open role, funding, season, move, migration | the trigger is public and dated |
| Ego | a small, relevant compliment, or a story where they win | you have a public rating, award, or review |
| Pattern interrupt | tone, format, or offer that breaks the inbox pattern | the inbox is full of your competitors' templates |
| Simplicity | the easier the ask, the more it happens; applies most to the CTA | always |
| Story | narrative lowers defences | audiences with imagination; not for formal professions |
| Autonomy | pressure creates resistance; humbling disclaimers disarm | formal audiences and follow-ups |

## Structure

Three parts. Nothing else.

1. **Hook.** The reason this recipient, today. One or two sentences.
2. **What it is.** One sentence, verb first, in everyday words, that says what you do. Without it the reader cannot tell whether you are a person, a bot, or an offshore call centre, and files you under spam.
3. **CTA.** One conversation ask that explains what happens when they say yes.

Length: 40-70 words. Three short paragraphs at most. Ultra-short variants of about 20 words work as pattern breakers and belong in every variant mix. Up to 90 words only with a written justification in the copy file.

## Openers

- Observation the recipient can verify in 30 seconds beats a claim. "Your line rings out after 18:00" beats "many businesses miss calls."
- Scenario beats statistic. A moment they recognise beats a percentage they doubt.
- Something you did beats something you assert. "We called on Tuesday at 9:12 and nobody picked up" beats "you probably miss calls."
- The test for line 1 is interchangeability, not wording. "I saw you are a partner of X" is on every banned list and still works when the detail is real and rare. The rule is: never an opener that would also be true for 10,000 other recipients.
- Generic pain openers ("Do you know the feeling when...") read as template. Cut them.

## The what-it-is sentence

Verb first. Everyday words. No superlatives.

- Bad: "We offer the leading AI-powered scheduling platform for SMBs in the region."
- Good: "We built a tool that answers your phone when nobody else can and books the job."

## CTA shapes

| Shape | Principle | Example |
|---|---|---|
| No-brainer | yes or no, costs no time | "Want me to write down how this runs at a shop your size?" |
| Lead magnet | give first, then ask; strongest when built for this recipient | "I ran your number through our check and wrote up what happened. Want it?" |
| The choice | autonomy plus a one-letter answer | "A) a live demo line, B) three example calls from your trade, C) pricing. Reply with the letter." |
| Disarming question | pressure removed, self-doubting | "Am I completely off here?" |

Rules:
- One CTA per message. A softer second ask in a P.S. is a test, not a default.
- No calendar link in touch 1. Reply with a word, a letter, or a call to a number.
- The CTA sentence carries its own context. "Try it once" is not a CTA. "Call this number and hear it answer, free, no signup" is.
- Campaigns with no CTA at all have performed well as pattern breakers. Worth one variant, never the control.

## Proof

- One proof point per message, local or same-trade beats large and famous.
- Every number has a source the recipient could ask for. Unsupported multipliers ("21x", "300 percent") are the worst performers we have seen, in every market.
- Quote a public review verbatim if you have one. A verbatim quote cannot be argued with.

## Variants

- 2-3 variants per step. More splits the sample below what you can learn from (500 sends per variant is the floor for reading anything).
- Each variant changes one thing on purpose: opener type, CTA shape, proof type, length. Not wording.
- Each variant carries a one-line hypothesis in the copy file. A variant without a hypothesis is noise.
- Mix types across the set: one ultra-short authority variant, one scenario variant, one proof-plus-CTA variant.
- Do not generate sentence-level permutations. Two or three clean variants beat 27 stitched ones.

## Deliverability hygiene inside the copy

- No links, no images, no attachments, no brand domain in touch 1. Say the company name, not the URL.
- No emoji, no capitals-only words, no symbols as CTAs ("reply with →").
- No spam-list vocabulary: free, guarantee, limited, act now, exclusive, winner.
- Keep spintax to the sentence level and to 2-3 options. Providers vary the text; you own the meaning.
- Follow-up in the same thread with an empty subject reads human and lifts replies.

## Follow-ups

See `follow-ups.md`. Short version: every follow-up is Different (new angle), Disarming (light, no neediness), Direct (no filler). It never restates step 1. It stands alone for a reader who never saw step 1. Two steps is the default for direct SMB email. Three to four is the ceiling for any email-led sequence.

## LinkedIn-first sequences

Where LinkedIn is available, it has produced several times the positive replies per thousand touches of email in our engagements, especially for engineering and leadership buyers. Shape:

1. Day 0: connection invite. Default is a bare invite with no note. The messaged invite is the challenger; test it, do not assume it.
2. On accept, day 0: one short message. The observation and the ask, under 40 words.
3. Day 2: a one-line bump with a new angle.
4. Day 3: a disarming "maybe I reached the wrong person, who owns this at {{companyName}}?" line.
5. Day 7: withdraw the invite if never accepted.
6. Email leg only for contacts who did not accept, and only with the verified-address filter.

Rules:
- Cap at 7 touches. Nothing past 7 adds quality.
- Hot leads (strong signal) get LinkedIn first because the signal justifies the handcrafted effort. Warm leads get email first because LinkedIn capacity is the bottleneck.
- Event or introduction anchors work on LinkedIn. Bulk email to an event list with no anchor does not.

## Voice override block

Every company gets a block in `context/company.md`. It wins over every default in this file.

```markdown
## Voice override
address: formal | informal          # per vertical if mixed; list the verticals
spelling: en-GB | en-US | de-DE
forbidden_punctuation: em dash, en dash [, hyphen as punctuation]
forbidden_words: leading, innovative, seamless, ...
founder_writes: step 1 LinkedIn note   # leave placeholders; never stage placeholders
signature: {{accountSignature}}        # never a hard-coded closing
sender_identity: local name, local number, local domain
allowed_proof: <list>                  # nothing outside this list
```

Some founders forbid every dash including the hyphen. Respect it. Rephrase "2-min video" as "2 minute video".

## Register: the slop gate

Model-flavoured or brochure-flavoured text is a hard gate, not a style preference. Before the rubric, run the nine-step check in `anti-slop-writing.md`. The short list: no dashes or substitute kickers, no triadic fragments, no "not just X, Y", no forced contrasts, no pivot pairs, no hook questions, no abstract noun stacks, no adjective spirals, no intensifiers, no opener or sign-off clichés, one claim per sentence, every specific claim verified, every general claim with its evidence next to it. A single hit fails the copy review.

## Language

- Write native. Translating copy from another language has produced zero positive replies in every attempt we have measured. If nobody on the team writes the language, do not ship in it.
- Everyday spoken word beats business word: "works" not "effective", "set up" not "implement", "problem" not "challenge", "calls nobody picks up" not "missed opportunities".
- Test: would you write this sentence to an acquaintance in a chat message? If not, rewrite.
- One address form per message and per pool.

## What we deliberately do not do

| Common advice | Why we skip it |
|---|---|
| Swearing, "unhinged" tone, bragging signatures | destroys trust with formal professions; use with extreme restraint in informal trades only |
| Slang power-word lists | untranslatable; keep the principle (everyday words), skip the list |
| Naming a recipient's direct reports | small firms have no org chart; in the EU it reads as creepy and is data-protection sensitive |
| Funding, university, investor personalisation | does not exist for most SMBs; use public rating, opening hours, season, review text instead |
| Five or more follow-ups | steps past 2 add near-zero opportunities for direct SMB; past 4 for any email-led sequence |
| Longer emails when justified | measurably worse for SMB; 40-70 words stays |
| Two case studies in one mail | reads as an agency pitch; one local proof point |
| "91 percent read the P.S. first" | folklore; use a P.S. sparingly, never as dogma |
