# Anti-slop writing

Cold outreach written by a model, or by a person imitating one, has a recognisable texture. Readers spot it in the first line and stop reading. This reference names the patterns so you can delete them. It governs register and rhythm, not what you say. An explicit operator instruction ("make it punchy", "keep the fragment") wins over any rule here.

## Philosophy

1. **Humble, not salesy.** You are a capable person writing to a capable person. Not a brand talking to a market. Not a junior impressing a senior.
2. **Concrete over abstract.** A parcel, a Tuesday, a review, a phone that rings out. Not clarity, alignment, momentum, efficiency.
3. **One claim per sentence.** If a sentence carries two claims, split it or cut one.
4. **Truth gate.** A specific claim that is false is worse than a vague claim. "Your March review mentions a late delivery" must be true for this recipient. If you cannot verify the specific, say the general or say nothing.
5. **Evidence locality.** Every claim follows from evidence the reader can see or check within the message or within 30 seconds. "Shops your size lose two bookings a week to voicemail" needs the source in the next clause or it goes.

## Hard ban: dashes

No em dash, no en dash, no spaced hyphen, no colon or ellipsis doing the same job. The job in question is the appended kicker: a main clause followed by a punchline fragment. "We flag stuck parcels, the day they stall." is the same move with a comma. Write two sentences or cut the kicker.

## Banned rhythm patterns

| Pattern | Example | Why it reads as slop |
|---|---|---|
| Triadic fragments | "One tool. One inbox. One answer." | cadence pretending to be content |
| Alliterative verb stacks | "spot, sort, and solve" | sound over sense |
| "Not just X, Y" and "more than just X" | "not just a dashboard, a decision engine" | manufactured upgrade |
| Forced contrast | "Less chasing. More closing." | slogan, no claim |
| Pivot pairs | "It's not about speed. It's about trust." | denies a claim nobody made |
| Appended kicker | "We answer the phone. Every time." | see the dash ban; same move |
| Rhetorical build-up | "Sound familiar? Thought so." | scripted intimacy |

## Banned voice moves

| Move | Example | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| Abstract noun stacks | clarity, alignment, momentum, visibility, efficiency | the concrete thing the noun stands for |
| Adjective spirals | powerful, intuitive, seamless, effortless, robust | delete; describe what happens |
| Pseudo-koans | "The best follow-up is the one you never send." | delete |
| Manifesto voice | "because every shop deserves a phone that answers" | delete; say what the tool does |
| Hook questions | "What if you never missed a call again?" | an observation about this recipient |
| Hidden-truth intensifiers | actually, really, in fact, the truth is, honestly | delete and reread; the sentence is usually fine without it |
| Experiential clichés | "welcome to the future of", "experience X" | delete |
| Unearned "you" | "you know how frustrating it is when..." | only address what you can see about them |
| Filler sign-offs | "excited to connect", "looking forward to hearing from you" | your name |
| Opener clichés | "I hope this finds you well", "quick question", "just checking in", "circling back", "reaching out" | start with the observation |
| Meta-commentary | "I'll keep this short" | keep it short |
| Fake modesty | "I know you're busy, but" | delete |

## Positive rules

- Full sentences by default. A fragment is earned once per message, when the operator asks for it or the rhythm genuinely needs it.
- Concrete nouns and real numbers. "412 reviews" beats "hundreds of reviews".
- Direct address with action verbs. "Reply 'list' and I send it." Not "feel free to reach out if interested."
- One ask per email, stated explicitly, answerable in one word.
- Delete any line that exists only for cadence. Read the message without it. If nothing is lost, it stays deleted.
- Negation only when the contrasted claim carries content. "Not a call centre, a piece of software that runs on your number" is fine because the reader might have assumed a call centre. "Not just software" is not.
- Collegial register: capable to capable. Not corporate-formal ("we would be delighted"), not LinkedIn-deep ("here's what nobody tells you about").
- Present tense, active voice, short words.

## Two jobs: edit and detect

**Edit** is the default. Take the draft, make the minimum effective edit, return the draft plus a three-line "What changed". Preserve the writer's voice: their vocabulary, cadence, bluntness, humour, hedges, digressions. Leave a strong human sentence alone even if it breaks a rule here. Protect every specific fact and number. Prefer human subjects and active verbs ("we shipped it Tuesday", not "the decision emerged"). Make verbs do the work ("decided", not "made a decision"; "can", not "has the ability to"). Do not add claims, examples, statistics, quotes or opinions the writer did not supply; ask instead.

**Detect** runs when the operator asks whether a draft reads as machine-written, or before any variant is presented. Name each pattern from this file that appears, quote the offending line, and give the fix in a few words. Do not rewrite, do not score, do not guess authorship. Named patterns are evidence the operator can check; detectors guess. Offer the edit afterwards.

## Words to cut

Banned outright in outreach: delve, foster, leverage, utilize, facilitate, empower, streamline, robust, cutting-edge, paradigm shift, game changer, this changes everything, tapestry, realm, beacon, multifaceted, meticulous, intricate, paramount, transformative, elevate, embark, supercharge, harness, ever-evolving, seamless, unlock, navigate (unless a boat is involved).

Usually empty adverbs: just, literally, honestly, simply, actually, truly, fundamentally, importantly, crucially, inherently, inevitably. Cut them and reread. Keep one only when it carries real uncertainty or contrast the sentence loses without it.

Usually empty phrases: it's worth noting, it's important to note, at the end of the day, when it comes to, at its core, in today's world, in the age of, the reality is, the truth is, in terms of, with regard to, in order to, going forward, I wanted to reach out, I'd love to.

## Patterns to cut

| Pattern | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Binary contrast | "The problem isn't your team. It's your list." | "Your list is the problem." |
| Throat-clearing opener | "Here's the thing: most shops lose bookings after 6pm." | "Most shops lose bookings after 6pm." |
| Faux-insight setup | "What nobody tells you about missed calls is that they cost more than the call." | "A missed call costs more than the call." |
| Colon reveal | "The detail that makes it work: a second agent grades it." | "A second agent grades it, which is what makes it work." |
| Trailing analysis | "You added a booking page, highlighting your focus on convenience." | "You added a booking page, so customers can book at 11pm." |
| Importance puffery | "This marks a pivotal moment for your shop." | "This is your first paid channel." |
| Weasel attribution | "Studies show 70 percent of callers hang up." | Name the study or cut the number. |
| Fake-strong verb | "Our tool serves as a central hub for shipments." | "Our tool lists every shipment and its status." |
| Synonym cycling | "The agent reviews the draft. The assistant scores it. The tool suggests fixes." | "The agent reviews the draft, scores it, and suggests fixes." |
| Negative listing | "Not a CRM. Not a dialer. A phone that answers." | "Software that answers your phone." |
| Dramatic fragmentation | "That's it. That's the whole thing." | Delete. |
| Robotic rhythm | three sentences of identical shape in a row | vary length; or cut one |
| Rhetorical setup | "What if I told you…" / "Plot twist:" | State the point. |
| Fake-profound kicker | "Because the best call is the one you never miss." | Delete the line. End on the ask. |
| Summary recap | "In short, we help you answer more calls." | Delete. The reader was just there. |
| Formatting slop | emoji, bold mid-sentence, bullets in a 50-word email | plain sentences |

Cold-outreach additions on top of the general list: dash count is zero, not "one or two"; one ask per message; no "I hope this finds you well", "quick question", "just checking in", "circling back", "I'd love to", "excited to".

## Pre-send slop check

Run in order. Stop and fix at the first failure.

1. **Register.** Read it aloud. Would a capable peer say this to another capable peer? If it sounds like a brand, a keynote, or a LinkedIn post, rewrite.
2. **Truth.** Every specific claim about the recipient is verified against the record. No specific you cannot stand behind.
3. **Evidence locality.** Every other claim has its evidence in the same sentence or the next one.
4. **Dash count.** Zero. Including substitute punctuation doing the kicker job.
5. **Intensifiers.** Delete actually, really, in fact, truly, honestly, genuinely. Reread. Keep the deletion.
6. **Rhythm.** No triads, no alliterative stacks, no "not just", no forced contrasts, no pivots, no kickers.
7. **One claim.** Every sentence carries one claim. Split or cut the rest.
8. **Cut check.** Remove each line in turn. If nothing is lost, it stays removed.
9. **Salesy check.** Count adjectives and abstract nouns. More than two in the whole message: cut until two.

## Before and after

Fictional. Harbor Metrics (harbormetrics.example) flags stuck parcels for online shops. Recipient: Tom Hardt, owner of Juniper Bikes (juniperbikes.example).

### Slop version

Subject: `What if late deliveries were a thing of the past?`

> Hi Tom, hope this finds you well!
>
> Running an online bike shop isn't just about selling bikes. It's about trust. And nothing breaks trust faster than a parcel that goes silent.
>
> Harbor Metrics gives you clarity, control and confidence over every shipment. Powerful tracking. Proactive alerts. Happier customers.
>
> Less chasing. More riding.
>
> Would love to show you how. Excited to connect!

Failures: hook question, opener cliché, pivot pair, abstract noun stack, adjective spiral, triadic fragments, forced contrast, filler sign-off, no ask, no evidence, no specific about Tom.

### Fixed version

Subject: `tom, march review`

> Tom, one of your March reviews says the bike arrived "ten days late and nobody told me".
>
> We built a tool that spots a parcel the day it stalls and messages the customer before they write to you.
>
> Want the list of last month's late ones for Juniper Bikes? Reply "list". Reply "no" and I stop.
>
> Sam

55 words. One specific, verified. One what-it-is sentence. One ask, one word to answer, one word to opt out.

### Follow-up, slop version

> Just circling back on my last note. I know you're busy, but I really think this could be a game-changer for Juniper Bikes. Any thoughts?

### Follow-up, fixed version

> Wrong person, or wrong problem? If someone else looks after shipping at Juniper Bikes, a name is enough.

## Scope note

This reference governs register and patterns. It does not decide the angle, the lever, or the proof; those live in `copy-rules.md`. When an operator gives an explicit instruction that conflicts with a rule here, the instruction wins and the copy file records it.
